How to use it — the ten steps

The first three have nothing to do with the theme, and they are half the job.

How the job goes

  1. Decide what the site is for — who it is for, and what you want a visitor to do.
  2. Plan your pages. Five or six is plenty. That list becomes your menu.
  3. Gather your content first — the words for each page, your photos, your logo, two or three brand colours.
  4. Open index.html and click through the demo. Notice which layout suits each of your pages.
  5. Copy the folder and rename it after your site. Leave the original untouched.
  6. Keep the pages you need, delete the rest, duplicate one for any extra.
  7. Swap the words and pictures, one page at a time. Home first.
  8. Change the site name and the colours. One block at the top of theme.css controls the colours for every page.
  9. Open every page, click every link, try it on your phone.
  10. Upload the folder to any web host. Nothing to install.

Stuck on any step? Email support is included for 60 days. And if you would rather not do steps 5 to 10 at all, installation on your domain starts at £150.

What it isn't: a page builder, a CMS or a component framework. It's HTML and CSS you edit by hand — which is why there's no build step and nothing to keep updated. If that's not how you want to work, I'll install and customise it for you instead.

The mistakes it stops you making

  • Paid features unlocking for free. Anything the browser holds, a user can edit. Here the database is the only thing that decides who has paid, and only a signed message from Stripe is allowed to write to it. A tampered cookie unlocks nothing.
  • A customer who paid and can't get in. The worst billing bug there is: the money leaves, the upgrade doesn't happen, and you hear about it from an angry email days later. The webhook path here is the one that has already been run end to end.
  • An AI bill you find out about at month end. Connect a language model without limits and one loop or one abusive user writes you an invoice. Usage caps are built in rather than added after the first scare.
  • Accounts that vanish on deploy. A classic first-product disaster. This has been confirmed to survive redeployment with user data intact.
  • Changes that quietly break payments. 1,523 automated tests run on every change, so the billing path can't rot while you're busy building features.
